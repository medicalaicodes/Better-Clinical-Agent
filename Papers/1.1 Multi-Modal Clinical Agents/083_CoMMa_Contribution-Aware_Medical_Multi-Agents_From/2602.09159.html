CoMMa: Contribution-Aware Medical Multi-Agents
From A Game-Theoretic Perspective

Yichen Wu    Yujin Oh    Sangjoon Park    Kailong Fan    Dania Daye    Hana Farzaneh    Xiang Li    Raul Uppot    Quanzheng Li
Abstract

Recent multi-agent frameworks have broadened the ability to tackle oncology decision support tasks that require reasoning over dynamic, heterogeneous patient data. We propose Contribution-Aware Medical Multi-Agents (CoMMa), a decentralized LLM-agent framework in which specialists operate on partitioned evidence and coordinate through a game-theoretic objective for robust decision-making. In contrast to most agent architectures relying on stochastic narrative-based reasoning, CoMMa utilizes deterministic embedding projections to approximate contribution-aware credit assignment. This yields explicit evidence attribution by estimating each agent’s marginal utility, producing interpretable and mathematically grounded decision pathways with improved stability. Evaluated on diverse oncology benchmarks, including a real-world multidisciplinary tumor board dataset, CoMMa achieves higher accuracy and more stable performance than data-centralized and role-based multi-agents baselines. Source code will be released upon publication.

1 Introduction

Effective clinical decision support in oncology, such as treatment recommendation, prognosis estimation, and recurrence prediction, requires the seamless integration of longitudinal, heterogeneous data. In practice, this process is carried out through multidisciplinary tumor boards (Taylor et al., 2010; Patkar et al., 2011), where clinicians jointly discuss patient histories, medical imaging, pathology findings, and evolving laboratory markers. Replicating the collaborative reasoning process remains challenging for AI systems, as it demands robust reasoning over high-dimensional, dynamically structured evidence with clear attribution. Recently, large language model (LLM)-based multi-agents systems have emerged as a promising paradigm for this setting, distributing complex multimodal reasoning across specialized agents to better reflect multidisciplinary clinical decision-making (Chen et al., 2025a; Kim et al., 2024; Ferber et al., 2025; Han et al., 2025; Fallahpour et al., 2025).

Despite their promise, most medical multi-agent frameworks implement collaboration through role-play, assigning clinician-like personas such as oncologists or radiologists (Chen et al., 2025a; Kim et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2025). This setup often relies on long-form dialogue as the coordination channel, which increases interaction overhead and makes outcomes sensitive to conversational dynamics. More importantly, these systems are typically data-centralized, exposing all agents to the same full patient context. In this case, role specialization is largely semantic rather than structural, and it becomes difficult to isolate which evidence drives a decision or to assign responsibility when errors occur. As a result, current frameworks offer limited clinician-facing explainability and incur high inference cost, as shown in Figure 1(a) and (b).

To address these limitations, we propose Contribution-Aware Medical Multi-Agents (CoMMa), a framework that replaces role-based interaction with data-decentralized specialization. As illustrated in Figure 1(c), CoMMa partitions the clinical context into distinct information streams and assigns each to a dedicated agent, enforcing specialization by construction rather than by persona simulation. Agent communication is conducted through deterministic embedding projections instead of natural language dialogue, and the final clinical decision is formed by aggregating agent outputs with learnable contribution weights. This design improves scalability, reduces inference cost, and preserves clinical data privacy compared to data-centralized and role-based multi-agent systems, as summarized in Table 1.

Moreover, our CoMMa is grounded in cooperative game theory, where clinical decision-making is modeled as a coalitional process among agents. We adopt the principle of Shapley value (Shapley, 1953; Beechey et al., 2023) to regularize contribution learning, aligning each agent’s learned contribution weight with its estimated marginal utility. By minimizing the Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence between these quantities, CoMMa enables explicit and stable credit assignment, transforming multi-agent collaboration from a stochastic dialogue process into a structured and interpretable inference procedure. This contribution-aware formulation directly addresses the stability requirements of clinical decision support.

To verfiy the effectiveness of our CoMMa, we evaluate it across a diverse set of oncology benchmarks, with a real-world multidisciplinary tumor borad dataset as a key testbed. Experimental results demonstrate that CoMMa achieves state-of-the-art performance with significantly higher decision stability over data-centralized baselines.

Our core contributions are summarized as follows:

  • •

    We transition from stochastic text-based agent communication to a deterministic embedding framework, enabling a stable, locally fine-tunable architecture suitable for clinical deployment.

  • •

    We introduce a multi-agent modeling approach that ensures precise credit assignment across decentralized data streams, offering a transparent mechanism to evaluate the influence of specific data modalities.

  • •

    We demonstrate that CoMMa outperforms existing frameworks in predictive accuracy and interpretability across complex, multidisciplinary oncology scenarios, establishing a new state-of-the-art for agentic clinical decision support.

2 Related Works

2.1 Multi-Agents for Medical Applications

Recent studies have explored multi-agent LLM systems for collaborative clinical decision-making, where agents take specialized medical roles (Chen et al., 2025b; Tang et al., 2025; Zou and Topol, 2025; Johri et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025; Zhou et al., 2025; Almansoori et al., 2025; Fallahpour et al., 2025). MedAgents (Tang et al., 2024) frames problem-solving as a multi-physician debate with answer aggregation, while MDAgents (Kim et al., 2024) adaptively chooses single-versus multi-agent collaboration to balance accuracy and cost. Beyond free-form discussion, Multi-Agent Conversation (Chen et al., 2025a) structures deliberation to mimic multidisciplinary meetings, and AI Hospital (Fan et al., 2025) evaluates longitudinal reasoning in a multi-agent clinical simulation. However, these systems are primarily role-based: collaboration is mediated through narrative exchanges and the file decision is fused implicitly, making evidence-level contribution hard to quantify. In contrast, our contribution-aware framework explicitly attributes how each evidence source and agent influences the final prediction, enabling more controlled fusion and more reliable clinical decision outputs.

2.2 Game Theory in Clinical Decision-Making

Game theory provides mathematical frameworks for modeling strategic interactions among rational agents and has been widely applied in general-domain decision-making, including economics, management, defense, and computer science (Von Neumann and Morgenstern, 2007; Dutang et al., 2013; Ho et al., 2022; Parsons and Wooldridge, 2002; Lanctot et al., 2017). Despite its strong theoretical foundations, its adoption in clinical settings remains limited (Mendonça et al., 2020; Lau et al., 2023), particularly in oncology. Oncology decision-making relies on heterogeneous and interdependent evidence sources, where quantifying the contribution of each component is essential for interpretability and clinical reliability. Among cooperative game-theoretic approaches, the Shapley value (Shapley, 1953; Beechey et al., 2023) provides a principled and axiomatized solution for fair allocation of a total payoff among participating agents. In a clinical AI setting, the payoff corresponds to the final clinical decision or predictive outcome, while each agent represents a distinct modality or specialized source of clinical information. The Shapley value of an agent is defined as its expected marginal contribution, averaged over all possible coalitions, satisfying desirable payoff. To the best of our knowledge, this work is the first to incorporate Shapley value–based contribution modeling as a regularization mechanism in a multi-agent oncological tumor board framework.

3 Method

In this section, we provide a detailed schematic of CoMMa, as illustrated in Figure 2. We first introduce the deterministic embedding projections in Section 3.1, followed by the data-decentralized agent mixture in Section 3.2. Finally, in Section 3.3, we elaborate on the game-theoretic formulation that governs our contribution-aware multi-agent system.

3.1 Deterministic Embedding Projection

In clinical decision support, we consider the problem of predicting outcomes from heterogeneous, multimodal patient data 𝐱={𝐱1,𝐱2,…,𝐱N}\mathbf{x}=\{\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2},\dots,\mathbf{x}_{N}\}. Each text-based input 𝐱i\mathbf{x}_{i} distinct clinical information source, such as medical imaging reports, longitudinal laboratory measurements, or patient clinical history. To mitigate the stochasticity and hallucination-prone intermediate outputs of LLM, we introduce a deterministic projection operator FθF_{\theta} that maps each clinical text partition to a latent representation. Formally, for a given textual input 𝐱i\mathbf{x}_{i}, the projection is defined as:

𝐱ie​m​b=LLMfrozen​(𝐱i)​[<CLS>],Fθ​(𝐱i)=MLPθ​(𝐱ie​m​b),\begin{split}\mathbf{x}^{emb}_{i}=\text{LLM}_{\text{frozen}}(\mathbf{x}_{i})[\texttt{<CLS>}],\\ F_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{i})=\text{MLP}_{\theta}\left(\mathbf{x}^{emb}_{i}\right),\end{split} (1)

where LLMfrozen\text{LLM}_{\text{frozen}} serves as the fixed encoder for the input 𝐱\mathbf{x}, as detailed in Figure 2(a). To capture the salient clinical evidence without the overhead of full-sequence generation, we extract the hidden embedding of the terminal <CLS> (or |endoftext|) token embedding from the LLM’s final layer. This projected embedding 𝐱ie​m​b∈ℝD\mathbf{x}^{emb}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{D} functions as a DD-dimensional bottleneck representation. The parameter θ\theta the weights of a subsequent Multilayer Perceptron (MLP) projection that maps 𝐱ie​m​b\mathbf{x}^{emb}_{i} into a task-specific latent space. This operator constitutes the fundamental building block of each agent within our decentralized framework.

3.2 Data-Decentralized Agent Mixture

In this subsection, we contrast two distinct modeling paradigms: a standard data-centralized approach and our proposed decentralized, contribution-aware approach.

Data-Centralized Formulation. In this paradigm, a single global operator F𝜽F_{\boldsymbol{\theta}} observes the entire concatenated input at once, and multimodal clinical evidence is integrated implicitly through the model’s self-attention:

𝐲^=F𝜽​([𝐱𝟏;…;𝐱𝐍]),\hat{\mathbf{y}}=F_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{[\mathbf{x}_{1};\dots;{x}_{N}]}){\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\definecolor[named]{pgfstrokecolor}{rgb}{0,0,0}\pgfsys@color@gray@stroke{0}\pgfsys@color@gray@fill{0},} (2)

where 𝐲^∈ℝC\hat{\mathbf{y}}\in\mathbb{R}^{C} is the predicted logit vector across CC categories, [⋅;⋅][\cdot;\cdot] denotes string concatenation, and the parameters 𝜽\boldsymbol{\theta} are updated by minimizing the empirical risk ℒBCE​(𝐲^,𝐲)\mathcal{L}_{\text{BCE}}(\hat{\mathbf{y}},\mathbf{y}), where 𝐲∈ℝC\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{C} is the ground truth label. While effective, this formulation provides no explicit mechanism to isolate the effect of each information stream, making evidence attribution and responsibility assignment inherently ambiguous.

Data-Decentralized Formulation. In CoMMa, as illustrated in Figure 2(b), each agent ii applies the projection operator only to its designated partition 𝐱i\mathbf{x}_{i}, yielding a set of specialized embeddings:

𝐡i=f𝜽i​(𝐱i),\begin{split}\mathbf{h}_{i}=f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}}(\mathbf{x}_{i}),\end{split} (3)

where 𝜽i\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i} represents the learnable parameters of the ii-th agent’s projection head and 𝐡i∈ℝC\mathbf{h}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{C} is the agent’s logit vector over CC categories. By isolating information streams at the input level, we can explicitly measure each agent’s utility before final aggregation. The final prediction is then produced through a two-stage weighted aggregation.

3.2.1 Agent-Wise Aggregation.

The specialized embeddings {𝐡1,…,𝐡N}\{\mathbf{h}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{h}_{N}\} are fused using a learnable agent-decision matrix 𝐖∈ℝN×C\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times C}, where Wi,kW_{i,k} represents the contribution weight of agent ii for output dimension kk. The aggregated representation 𝐳A∈ℝD\mathbf{z}_{\mathrm{A}}\in\mathbb{R}^{D} is computed as:

zA,k=∑i=1NWi,k⋅hi,k,for ​k=1,…,C.z_{\mathrm{A},k}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}W_{i,k}\cdot h_{i,k},\quad\text{for }k=1,\dots,C. (4)

We normalize 𝐖\mathbf{W} so that ∑i,kWi,k=1\sum_{i,k}W_{i,k}=1 making Wi,kW_{i,k} directly interpretable as a per-class contribution weight. This aggregation yields a structured mixture over agent predictions, enabling explicit credit assignment across agents.

3.2.2 Global Mixture.

Decentralized specialization extracts complementary evidence, but clinical decisions still require a unified patient-level summary. We therefore introduce a global mixture module that combines the aggregated representations 𝐳A\mathbf{z}_{A} with a global clinical anchor 𝐱global\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{global}}. The final prediction 𝐲^∈ℝC\hat{\mathbf{y}}\in\mathbb{R}^{C} is given by,

𝐲^=MLPϕ​(w~A⋅𝐳A+w~G⋅f𝜽g​l​o​b​a​l​(𝐱global)),\hat{\mathbf{y}}=\text{MLP}_{\phi}(\tilde{w}_{\mathrm{A}}\cdot\mathbf{z}_{\mathrm{A}}+\tilde{w}_{\mathrm{G}}\cdot f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{global}}\left(\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{global}}\right)), (5)

where MLPϕ\text{MLP}_{\phi} denotes a task-specific projection head, and {w~A,w~G}\{\tilde{w}_{\mathrm{A}},\tilde{w}_{\mathrm{G}}\} are learnable mixture weights. Here, we define f𝜽g​l​o​b​a​lf_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{global}} as the global agent, which operates on the global anchor 𝐱global\mathbf{x}_{\text{global}}. This anchor is selected as the most comprehensive modality available within the dataset 𝐱\mathbf{x} (e.g., the primary clinical history or the concatenated entire input sequence) to provide a unified reference. Additional details are provided in Section A.1. By synthesizing these streams, the mixture mechanism leverages heterogeneous, modality-specific features while ensuring they remain anchored to a global clinical grounding.

3.3 Contribution-aware Multi-Agents

To ensure the agent-decision matrix 𝐖\mathbf{W} provides a faithful representation of clinical evidence, we reformulate the multi-agent collaboration as a cooperative coalitional game. In this setting, the predictive performance serves as the game’s reward function, and we aim to align the learned weights WiW_{i} with the agents’ respective Shapley values, which represent the only fair distribution of credit based on marginal utility. The final optimization step integrates the supervised classification signal with the agentic objectives defined as:

ℒtotal=ℒBCE​(𝐲^,𝐲)+λpg​ℒpg+λshap​ℒshap,\mathcal{L}_{\text{total}}=\mathcal{L}_{\text{BCE}}(\hat{\mathbf{y}},\mathbf{y})+\lambda_{\mathrm{pg}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{pg}}+\lambda_{\mathrm{shap}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{shap}}, (6)

where ℒBCE\mathcal{L}_{\text{BCE}} is the standard binary cross-entropy loss for multi-label classification, ℒpg\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{pg}} is a policy-gradient term that updates 𝐖\mathbf{W} using advantage signals to upweight agent-class pairs with positive marginal utility, and ℒshap\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{shap}} regularizes 𝐖\mathbf{W} toward Shapley-based credit assignment. We defer the definitions of ℒpg\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{pg}} and ℒshap\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{shap}} to the following subsections. In this setting, the model is prevented from collapsing into a state where a single agent disproportionately influences the output without demonstrably improving the objective.

3.3.1 Agent-wise Reward and Advantage

The first step in calculating these auxiliary objectives is to quantify the individual utility of each agent. The credit assignment mechanism utilizes agent-wise reward vectors 𝐫i′∈ℝC\mathbf{r}^{\prime}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{C}. The reward for agent ii on dimension kk is defined by the marginal reduction in ℒkB​C​E\mathcal{L}^{BCE}_{k} achieved by its inclusion:

ri,k′=ℒkB​C​E​(𝐲^−i,𝐲)−ℒkB​C​E​(𝐲^,𝐲),r^{\prime}_{i,k}=\mathcal{L}^{BCE}_{k}(\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{-i},\mathbf{y})-\mathcal{L}^{BCE}_{k}(\hat{\mathbf{y}},\mathbf{y}), (7)

where 𝐲^−i\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{-i} is the prediction generated when excluding agent ii from the coalition. To reduce variance in the gradient estimation, we define the advantage vector 𝐀i=𝐫i′−𝐛\mathbf{A}_{i}=\mathbf{r}^{\prime}_{i}-\mathbf{b}, where 𝐛=1N​∑j=1N𝐫j′\mathbf{b}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mathbf{r}^{\prime}_{j} serves as the baseline.

3.3.2 Policy-Gradient Loss

Using the advantage vector 𝐀i\mathbf{A}_{i} derived above, we optimize the agent-decision matrix 𝐖\mathbf{W} (introduced in Section 3.2.1). The policy-gradient term ℒpg\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{pg}} encourages the model to increase the contribution weights for agent-dimension pairs that yield a high positive advantage. This loss is computed by summing the log-probability of the weights modulated by their respective advantages:

ℒpg=−𝔼​[∑i=1N∑k=1Clog⁡(Wi,k)⋅Ai,k],\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{pg}}=-\mathbb{E}\!\left[\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{k=1}^{C}\log(W_{i,k})\cdot A_{i,k}\right], (8)

where Ai,kA_{i,k} is the kk-th component of 𝐀i\mathbf{A}_{i}.

3.3.3 Shapley Regularization Loss

While the policy-gradient objective ℒpg\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{pg}} enables the model to adapt to empirical utility, it is inherently susceptible to high-variance and stochastic reward fluctuations due to sample-wise advantage estimation. To mitigate this instability, we introduce a regularization term ℒshap\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{shap}} grounded in Shapley value (Shapley, 1953; Beechey et al., 2023).

We define the target as the C-dimensional Shapley matrix 𝚽∈ℝN×C\mathbf{\Phi}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times C}, where Φi,k\Phi_{i,k} quantifies the contribution of agent ii to decision dimension kk. Specifically, Φi,k\Phi_{i,k} is approximated via Monte Carlo estimation of the Shapley value over MM random coalitions S⊆𝒩∖{i}S\subseteq\mathcal{N}\setminus\{i\}:

Φi,k≈1M​∑m=1M[ℒkB​C​E​(Sm,𝐲)−ℒkB​C​E​(Sm∪{i},𝐲)]+.\Phi_{i,k}\approx\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\left[\mathcal{L}^{BCE}_{k}(S_{m},\mathbf{y})-\mathcal{L}^{BCE}_{k}(S_{m}\cup\{i\},\mathbf{y})\right]_{+}. (9)

Here, ℒkB​C​E​(S,𝐲)\mathcal{L}^{BCE}_{k}(S,\mathbf{y}) denotes the BCE loss for output dimension kk evaluated using only the agents in coalition SS, and ℒkB​C​E​(S∪{i},𝐲)\mathcal{L}^{BCE}_{k}(S\cup\{i\},\mathbf{y}) is the corresponding loss after adding agent ii. The loss difference therefore captures the marginal utility of agent ii relative to the baseline coalition SS. This construction enables fine-grained credit assignment by explicitly measuring how each agent improves task performance across output dimensions. The resulting Shapley matrix 𝚽\mathbf{\Phi} is normalized such that ∑i,kΦi,k=1\sum_{i,k}\Phi_{i,k}=1.

Then, the Shapley regularization loss is defined as the Kullback–Leibler divergence between 𝐖\mathbf{W} and 𝚽\mathbf{\Phi}, computed by summing over all components:

ℒshap=𝔼​[∑i=1N∑k=1CWi,k​log⁡(Wi,kΦi,k)].\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{shap}}=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{k=1}^{C}W_{i,k}\log\left(\frac{W_{i,k}}{\Phi_{i,k}}\right)\right]. (10)

This term regularizes 𝐖\mathbf{W} by pulling it towards the estimated contribution 𝚽\mathbf{\Phi}, thereby enforcing consistency between learned weights and cooperative game-theoretic credit assignment, as illustrated in Figure 2(c). To improve distributional stability during training, we maintain an exponential moving average (EMA) of the Shapley matrix. More detailed derivation of the Shapley approximation scheme, along with a formal comparison to the advantage vector 𝐀\mathbf{A}, is provided in Section A.2.

4 Experimental Results

4.1 Datasets

To evaluate the efficacy of the proposed framework within oncological decision-making settings, we conduct experiments across two distinct data sources. 1) HCC Tumorboard, a curated real-world clinical tumor board dataset, and 2) MTBBench, a public molecular tumor board benchmark (Vasilev et al., 2025). Further details on multimodal data variables and partitioning information are provided in Section A.1.

4.2 Implementation Details

We implement CoMMa using LangGraph (Chase, 2022) with Llama3.1-70B-Instruct (Grattafiori et al., 2024) as the backbone model for all experiments. We train separate model for each dataset. On the HCC Tumorboard dataset, we train for 1000 epochs with a learning rate of 5×10−55\times 10^{-5}. On MTBBench dataset, we train for 30 epochs with a learning rate of 5×10−35\times 10^{-3}. In both settings, we use five agents, batch size of 4, and set the regularization weights to λpg=1\lambda_{\textrm{pg}}=1 and λshap=10\lambda_{\textrm{shap}}=10. We use two NVIDIA A100 80GB GPUs for training each model. In addition, we compare the computational complexity and inference cost of our model with those of the baseline methods in Section A.3.

4.3 Evaluation Methods

For evaluation, we report decision category-wise Area Under the ROC Curve (AUC) and Accuracy. AUC quantifies a model’s discriminative ability to distinguish positive from negative outcomes. Accuracy is computed as,

Accuracy=1N​∑i=1N𝕀​(𝕀​(y^i,k>tk)=yi,k).\mathrm{Accuracy}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{I}\!\left(\mathbb{I}\!\left(\hat{y}_{i,k}>t_{k}\right)=y_{i,k}\right). (11)

where NN denotes total number of evaluation dataset, tkt_{k} denotes the class-specific decision threshold determined on the training set, y^i,k{\hat{y}}_{i,k} is the predicted logit for decision category kk, and yi,k{y}_{i,k} is the corresponding ground-truth label. Due to the limited sample size of the multidisciplinary cohorts, we employ a robust evaluation framework with validation repeated across three random seeds. To mitigate sampling bias, label distributions are balanced during the training–evaluation splits. Model stability and performance are assessed by reporting the mean AUC and accuracy across all experimental runs.

4.4 Baseline Methods and Evaluation Methods

We evaluate our method against three categories of baseline approaches. For locally trainable baselines optimized for categorical and structured data, we employ XGBoost (Chen, 2016), LGBMClassifier (Ke et al., 2017), and CatBoost (Prokhorenkova et al., 2018). These locally trainable models operate exclusively on structured inputs, therefore, all clinical information is formatted as tabular features for these baselines, whereas our model consumes purely textual input. For non-trainable online in-context learning baselines, we use GPT-4.1, which also serves as the backbone for all non-trainable online multi-agent baselines. From MDAgents (Kim et al., 2024), we adopt the Basic setting, where a single agent performs in-context learning without explicit multi-agent interaction. For in-context learning, the entire training dataset with paired ground-truth annotations is provided as context. For non-trainable online multi-agent baselines, we include MDAgents (Kim et al., 2024) and MAC (Chen et al., 2025a), which simulates structured tumor board discussions. In MDAgents, the Intermediate setting induces discussions among five domain-specific specialists, while the Advanced setting organizes specialists into multiple tumor board teams and facilitates inter-team deliberation. In MAC, a single supervisor agent coordinates discussions among four doctor agents. We follow the formatted text prompts provided in the baseline models, with slight modifications to incorporate task-specific instructions. For all multi-agent baselines, GPT-4.1 is consistently used as the underlying agent model to ensure a fair comparison.

4.5 Results

4.5.1 Analysis on HCC Tumorboard

On the HCC Tumorboard treatment recommendation task, CoMMA achieves the strongest overall performance among all compared methods. As shown in Table 2, CoMMa attains an AUC of approximately 0.759 and an accuracy of tbd, substantially outperforming both locally trainable classical baselines and non-trainable LLM-based approaches. Compared to traditional machine learning models such as XGBoost, LightGBM, and CatBoost, CoMMa demonstrates a clear advantage in capturing complex, heterogeneous clinical signals. Moreover, CoMMA also outperforms GPT-4.1 in-context learning and prior online multi-agent baselines, indicating that narrative discussion or retrieval-augmented prompting alone does not provide sufficiently reliable coordination for treatment recommendation. In contrast, CoMMa optimizes trainable multi-agent collaboration with a deterministic, contribution-aware credit assignment objective, explicitly rewarding agents based on marginal utility rather than stochastic dialogue heuristics. This yields more stable aggregation of evidence across decentralized partitions and translates into consistent improvements in recommendation quality, while providing agent-level attribution that is better aligned with clinical interpretability requirements. Detailed class-wise performance is provided in Section A.4.

We further qualitatively compare agentic framework responses in Figure 3. Single-agent and role-based multi-agent methods generate verbose textual rationales that are often misaligned with clinical context. In contrast, CoMMa provides quantitative attribution of agent contributions within standard decision logic, resulting in superior predictive performance. Although CoMMa does not produce fine-grained narratives, it yields compact, structured outputs that support clearer prediction. Additional response comparisons are provided in Section A.5.

4.5.2 Analysis on MTBBench

On MTBBench, we evaluate tumor progression and recurrence prediction, which require consolidating heterogeneous and longitudinal clinical evidence into a single outcome. As shown in Table 2, online LLM baselines and prior multi-agent discussion methods perform poorly and vary substantially across agent configurations, indicating fragile coordination and noisy evidence aggregation. In contrast, CoMMa achieves the best performance, attaining accuracies of 0.697 and 0.818 for progression and recurrence prediction, respectively. We attribute the gains to trainable multi-agent collaboration with deterministic, contribution-aware credit assignment, which leads to more stable and accurate outcome prediction under the challenging MTBBench setting.

4.5.3 Ablation Study

We conduct ablation studies to disentangle the effects of CoMMa’s core components in Table 3. We first compare the single-agent stochastic narrative baseline (a) with deterministic embeddings (b), observing a 4% absolute improvement in accuracy. Beyond improved performance, deterministic embeddings enable gradient-based optimization and class-wise logit outputs, allowing AUC-based evaluation, which is infeasible with narrative generation alone. We then scale the number of agents from one (b) to five data-decentralized agents without explicit coordination (c). This setting yields a notable improvement in AUC, while accuracy remains comparable. However, introducing a simple mixture via the agent–decision matrix (d) results in a slight performance decrease. These findings suggest that, even with decentralized agents, naive mixture weights are insufficient to fully exploit multi-agent reasoning and instead tend to overfit the training data. In contrast, CoMMa achieves a pronounced improvement in both AUC and accuracy relative to (d). This gain highlights the importance of contribution-aware learning over data-specified agents, as it explicitly models each agent’s marginal utility rather than treating agents as a weighted ensemble module. We further visualize the learned weight comparisons influenced by the Shapley matrix regularization in Figure 4. As training progresses, the initial agent-decision weights (𝐖\mathbf{W}) become closer to the contribution-aware Shapley matrix (𝚽\mathbf{\Phi}) than in the ablated setting. This indicates that disproportionate influence from any single agent is suppressed unless it contributes meaningfully to the objective. Finally, when replacing the agent backbone with a smaller LLM (Llama3-8B-Instruct) (e), performance decreases relative to the larger model. Notably, however, accuracy remains higher than that of the GPT-4.1 narrative baseline, suggesting that even smaller models, when embedded within a deterministic projection and contribution-aware framework, can outperform large-scale generative approaches, highlighting CoMMa’s potential for efficient and scalable clinical deployment.

5 Conclusion, Limitations and Future Work

In this study, we proposed CoMMa, a contribution-aware medical multi-agent framework that that replaces stochastic dialogue with deterministic specialization and structured credit assignment. Evaluations on oncology benchmarks demonstrate that CoMMa significantly improves predictive stability over centralized and narrative-based baselines. Moreover, CoMMa demonstrates that locally deployed architectures can achieve superior performance without relying on costly cloud-based inference or external data transfer. This marks a important step toward high-performance clinical AI in resource-constrained, privacy-sensitive settings.

Despite these strengths, our evaluation is currently constrained to specific oncology cohorts, reflecting the intrinsic scarcity and curation challenges of diverse real-world tumor board data. Furthermore, achieving granular reasoning remains challenging when processing highly clustered information across modalities. Future research will prioritize increasing data scale and disentangling data representations to enhance the resolution and minimize information entanglement. In addition, we will continue to address the limitations of the role-based multi-agents by further strengthening credit assignment principles to better capture the complex dynamics of oncology decision-making. Finally, the current CoMMa instantiation is evaluated on tasks cast as discrete multiple-choice classification with clear ground-truth labels, which does not fully reflect the open-ended, often ambiguous nature of real-world multidisciplinary decision-making. Extending contribution-aware multi-agent modeling to settings involving free-form queries, partially observed or uncertain outcomes, and inherently ambiguous decision spaces represents an important direction for future research.

Impact Statement

Our framework introduces explicit game-theoretic attribution to quantify data contributions, mitigating risks associated with hallucinations and reasoning errors in clinical LLMs. By enabling a locally deployable multi-agent system, it supports privacy-preserving clinical AI without reliance on costly online inference. We believe this research direction promotes more equitable clinical AI applications.

A.1 Dataset Details.

A.1.1 HCC Tumorboard

HCC Tumorboard dataset comprises a total of 116 patients diagnosed with primary Hepatocellular Carcinoma (HCC) at Massachusetts General Hospital (MGH), Boston, USA (Daye et al., 2024). The data collected for this study has been ethically approved by the IRB of the Department of Radiology at MGH (IRB #2022P001512). The study cohort includes patients who had a confirmed diagnosis of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) with no other concurrent malignancies, were reviewed by the multidisciplinary tumor board at MGH between August 2017 and August 2019, and were 18 years of age or older. Each case was reviewed by a multidisciplinary tumor board to establish the consensus treatment recommendation, serving as the ground truth labels. We filter out cases that lack recommendations in all categories. For the HCC dataset, we adopt a 75 / 25 % train–test split. The ground truth label is defined by 8 discrete treatment options: (A) Transcatheter Arterial Chemoembolization (TACE), (B) Ablation, primarily Microwave Ablation (MWA), (C) Radioembolization, also termed Selective Internal Radiation Therapy (SIRT), (D) Radiotherapy, (E) Surgical resection, (F) Liver transplant, (G) Chemotherapy, and (J) Palliative. The clinical data for each instance comprises 24 multimodal variables represented in a textual format, which extracted from the longitudinal clinical records and Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) scans. A.1.2 MTBBench

MTBBench dataset (Vasilev et al., 2025) is an agentic benchmark designed to evaluate molecular tumor board-style reasoning that requires integrating multimodal evidence and longitudinal patient context. t contains 66 patient cases and 573 expert-validated question-answer pairs across two tracks. The multimodal track includes 26 head and neck cancer cases, with roughly 40 modality-specific files per patient, such as H and E slides, IHC images, and hematology reports, resulting in 390 questions. The longitudinal track includes 40 cases with about five structured files per patient and 183 questions focused on time-dependent outcomes, including recurrence and progression. In our experiments, we focus on prognosis prediction tasks for tumor recurrence and progression. Each case consists of a paired question and a binary classification label. For the MTBBench dataset, we adopt a 80 / 20 % train–test split. We further provide an exemplar case illustrating the formation of the MTBBench dataset in Table 5.

A.2.1 Shapley Value Approximation Strategy

Exact computation of the Shapley value is computationally expensive, especially as the number of agents NN increases. To achieve a tractable yet accurate approximation, we employ Monte Carlo sampling over M=2N/2M=2^{N/2} randomly sampled marginal contributions, where NN denotes the total number of agents. This strategy substantially reduces computational complexity while preserving the statistical fidelity of the Shapley estimation. Furthermore, although the classical Shapley value admits both positive and negative contributions, we apply a rectified linear unit (ReLU) operator [⋅]+[\cdot]_{+} to enforce non-negativity. This modification ensures that the resulting Shapley estimates form a valid target distribution for the KL divergence regularization in Equation (10), and that agents are credited exclusively for reducing the task loss.

A.2.2 Comparison to Advantage Vector

Unlike the advantage vector 𝐀i\mathbf{A}_{i}, as introduced in Section 3.3.1, which only considers the marginal impact of removing agent ii from the full coaliations 𝒩\mathcal{N}, Shapley value defined in Equation (12) evaluates the contribution of agent ii across a diverse set of sub-coalitions S⊆𝒩∖{i}S\subseteq\mathcal{N}\setminus\{i\}. of varying cardinalities. Consequently, the resulting Shapley matrix 𝚽\mathbf{\Phi} captures an expected marginal contribution that explicitly accounts for inter-agent interactions and dependencies, rather than being dominated by sample-specific or coalition-dependent fluctuations.
